## The Problem

Amazon committed to deploying 100,000 custom Rivian electric delivery vans — the largest EV fleet order in history. But going from a press release to vehicles on the road surfaced a cascade of product challenges that no one had solved before.

The pilot fleet of 4 vehicles revealed a fundamental gap: **there was no integrated product layer** connecting Rivian's vehicle systems to Amazon's logistics platform. Each market had different charging infrastructure, route density, driver training requirements, and regulatory constraints. The existing fleet management tools were built for gas-powered vehicles and couldn't handle EV-specific concerns like battery state-of-charge, charging schedules, or range anxiety routing.

## Why It Mattered

This wasn't a technology experiment — it was a core operational bet. Amazon's Climate Pledge depended on this fleet. Every week of delay meant:

- **Operational cost:** Gas vehicles continued running routes that EVs could serve more cheaply
- **Climate commitment risk:** Public pledge to be net-zero carbon by 2040, with last-mile delivery as a key lever
- **Competitive signal:** Amazon needed to prove that EVs could work at logistics scale, not just for consumer commutes
- **Driver experience:** Delivery Associates were being asked to adopt unfamiliar vehicles without adequate tooling

### Key Tradeoffs

- **Speed vs. completeness:** Ship a minimum viable fleet management layer fast, or wait for a comprehensive platform?
- **Standardization vs. market fit:** One playbook for all markets, or customized per-market configurations?
- **Build vs. integrate:** Build EV-specific tools from scratch, or extend existing Amazon fleet tools?

## Key Decisions

### Decision 1: API-First Integration with Rivian

**Options considered:**
| Option | Pros | Cons |
|--------|------|------|
| Custom middleware layer | Full control, Amazon-specific optimizations | 6+ month build, maintenance burden |
| Direct Rivian API integration | Faster, leverage Rivian's existing telemetry | Dependency on Rivian's roadmap, API stability risk |
| Hybrid: API integration + Amazon abstraction layer | Best of both, future-proofed | More upfront architecture work |

**Decision:** Hybrid approach. Defined the API requirements and acceptance criteria with Rivian's engineering team, creating an abstraction layer that let Amazon's systems consume Rivian vehicle data without tight coupling. This meant if Rivian's APIs changed, only the adapter layer needed updating.

## Reflection

**What worked:**
- The hybrid API approach gave us speed without creating a brittle integration. When Rivian pushed firmware updates, our abstraction layer absorbed the changes.
- Market-by-market rollout let us learn from each deployment and improve the playbook.

**What I'd do differently:**
- **Start driver research earlier.** We underestimated how much the DA experience differed between gas and electric vehicles. Starting user research in Month 1 instead of Month 3 would have surfaced UX issues sooner.
- [PLACEHOLDER: Second lesson learned]
- **Build a charging simulation tool.** We validated charging infrastructure manually in each market. A simulation tool could have predicted charging conflicts before they happened.

**What this taught me about PM work:**
This was the project that crystallized my product leadership philosophy: **hardware-software integration challenges are fundamentally product problems, not engineering problems.** The technical work of connecting APIs is straightforward. The hard part is defining what data matters, for whom, and when — and making tradeoff decisions when the hardware team and the software team have conflicting priorities.
